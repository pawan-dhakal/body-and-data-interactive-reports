Beyond Access

Women and Queer Persons with Disabilities Expressing Self and Exploring Sexuality Online

Digital technologies are often celebrated as neutral equalizers. However, for women and queer persons with disabilities in Nepal, accessing the internet requires overcoming physical, linguistic, financial, and patriarchal gatekeeping. This groundbreaking intersectional study documents how disabled bodies navigate digital spaces to explore pleasure, desire, romance, and bodily autonomy.

Headline Finding

"Society views persons with disabilities through an oppressive binary: either as completely 'asexual' beings needing paternalistic protection, or as 'hypersexual' anomalies to be policed. Despite online harassment and surveillance, the internet remains an indispensable lifeline for disabled women and queer people to assert bodily autonomy, express desire, and find community."

Methodological Grounding

Feminist Ethical Principles & Operational Definitions

"Research on sexuality, disability, and digital technology demands non-extractive feminist ethics. We prioritize participant consent, narrative sovereignty, confidentiality, and the centering of lived experience."

Younikta (यौनिकता — Sexuality)

An expansive feminist concept encompassing desire, pleasure, romantic attraction, bodily intimacy, identity, and personal relationships, moving far beyond mere biological reproduction.

Social Model of Disability

Recognizes that disability is created by systemic social, physical, and digital barriers—such as inaccessible apps and social stigma—rather than an individual's medical impairment.

Bodily Autonomy

The fundamental right of every individual to make decisions about their own body, medical choices, relationships, and digital representations without guardian interference.

Inspiration Porn

The objectification of disabled people for the emotional benefit of non-disabled audiences, portraying ordinary daily activities as extraordinary heroism.

Core Framework

A Spectrum of Access: Seven Progressive Layers

Digital inclusion is not binary. To fully exercise rights online, a person with disabilities must successfully navigate seven sequential layers of infrastructure, software, literacy, and social autonomy.

1
Layer 1: A Device of Your Own Personal ownership vs. shared family hardware

Only 32% of disabled women in Nepal own a dedicated smartphone. Most must borrow devices from male family members or parents, eliminating all privacy for personal browsing and intimate communication.

2
Layer 2: Data You Can Afford Telecom tariff barriers & voice pack limitations

High mobile data package costs disproportionately exclude disabled persons who face higher unemployment rates. Telecom concessions exist for voice calls but exclude high-bandwidth data required for video calls by Deaf users.

3
Layer 3: An Interface That Works with Your Body Screen reader compatibility, alt-text, captions

Government portals and social platforms routinely lack alternative text for images, present unreadable image-based CAPTCHAs, and fail WCAG 2.1 AA screen reader navigation standards.

4
Layer 4: Content in Your Language Nepali speech synthesis & sign language video

Major screen readers (TalkBack, NVDA, VoiceOver) lack high-quality native Nepali text-to-speech synthesis, forcing blind users to read Romanized Nepali or navigate in English.

5
Layer 5: Skills and Someone to Ask Assistive tech literacy without condescension

Peer support networks are vital. When software updates break screen reader shortcuts, blind users rely on disability WhatsApp groups rather than tech support.

6
Layer 6: A Household That Lets You Use It Overprotection, device gatekeeping, and moral policing

Families routinely confiscate smartphones from disabled daughters under the pretext of 'safety', monitoring their chat logs and preventing romantic interactions.

7
Layer 7: A Space Where You Are Not Harassed Freedom from ableist slurs, fetishization, and extortion

Disabled women who assert visible online presence encounter fetishizing comments, fake proposals, and extortion exploitation targeting their perceived vulnerability.

Chapter I

I. Sexuality and Bodies of PwD — A Culture of Silence

"Society views disability through a charity lens that completely erases sexuality. Disabled women are treated as perpetual children without sexual feelings, or as hypersexual burdens whose desires must be medicated and restrained."

In South Asian patriarchal culture, a woman's social value is inextricably tied to her 'marriageability' (bibahikta). Disabled women are constructed as unmarriageable, which leads families to actively suppress any open discussion of their romantic desires, reproductive health, or sexual pleasure.

Discriminatory laws codify this infantilization: Article 71.2 of the Civil Code 2017 permits the annulment of marriage if a spouse has a physical or mental impairment not disclosed prior to marriage, while Section 6 of the Individual Privacy Act 2018 grants legal guardians full access to personal health records of persons with disabilities2.

Chapter III

III. The Cost of Being a Role Model & Four Arenas of Policing

"Disabled women who achieve public prominence face intense pressure to remain 'flawless, modest, and asexual role models'. Any expression of sexuality is treated as a betrayal of the disability movement."

Arena 01

Family & Caregivers

Constant surveillance of call logs, demanding passwords, and moral reprimands for late-night chats.

Arena 02

Social Media Commenters

Trolling posts with ableist slurs, mockery of physical appearance, or backhanded compliments ("You're pretty for a disabled girl").

Arena 03

Disability NGO Leadership

Senior male leaders policing young female members' social media photos and discouraging romantic relationships.

Arena 04

Feminist & Queer Spaces

Tokenistic inclusion without physical or digital accessibility accommodations for sign language and screen readers.

Chapter IV

IV. Lack of Safe Spaces & 6-Tier Violence Ladder

Online violence against women and queer persons with disabilities escalates along a spectrum from subtle microaggressions to severe criminal extortion.

L1
Level 1: Everyday Microaggressions & Disbelief "You don't look disabled" · Invalidating lived identity

Backhanded compliments questioning the authenticity of invisible disabilities, demanding medical proof in public comment sections.

L2
Level 2: Unpaid Emotional Labor on Demand Entitled demands for accessibility education

Strangers expecting disabled creators to provide free counseling, disability awareness, and traumatic personal disclosures on demand.

L3
Level 3: Fetishization & Voyeurism Targeted fetishizing requests exploiting impairments

Men sending unsolicited sexual messages asking to see amputated limbs, blind eyes, or wheelchair transfers under the guise of curiosity.

L4
Level 4: Deceptive Romance & Manipulation Exploiting isolation for financial and sexual gain

Perpetrators feigning romantic love to extract intimate images, loans, or mobile recharge cards from socially isolated disabled women.

L5
Level 5: Doxxing & Non-Consensual Image Sharing Forwarding intimate screenshots to family and employers

Distributing private photos into community WhatsApp groups to destroy the victim's social standing and force familial isolation.

L6
Level 6: Criminal Cyber Extortion & Blackmail Demanding ransom under threat of viral defamation

Hacking accounts, creating morph photos, and demanding large sums of money, leaving victims without accessible police reporting mechanisms.

Advocacy Roadmap

V. Recommendations across Five Sectors

A multi-stakeholder framework for ensuring dignity, accessibility, and bodily autonomy online.

01 Enforce WCAG 2.1 AAA on All Public Portals

Mandate that all government websites, digital ID portals, and public services comply with international screen reader accessibility standards and provide sign language video alternatives.

02 Subsidize Assistive Hardware & High-Speed Data

Provide subsidized smartphones, braille displays, and zero-rated data packages for persons with disabilities through the Rural Telecommunications Development Fund (RTDF).

03 Integrate SRHR and Digital Rights into Disability Advocacy

Move beyond charity and medical rehabilitation models to actively advocate for bodily autonomy, sexuality education, and online safety for disabled women and queer members.

04 Develop Open-Source Nepali Text-to-Speech Engines

Invest in building high-quality, open-source Nepali speech synthesis tools and eliminate inaccessible visual CAPTCHAs from authentication systems.

05 Dismantle Ableist Stigma & Pity Tropes

Reject 'inspiration porn' and recognize persons with disabilities as autonomous sexual and emotional beings entitled to privacy and romantic relationships.

06 Ensure Intersectional Disability Inclusion

Feminist and queer organizations must ensure their conferences, campaigns, and digital platforms provide accessible physical venues, sign interpreters, and alt-text.
