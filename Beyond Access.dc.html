Beyond Access

Women and queer persons with disabilities expressing self and exploring sexuality online

Having a phone is not access. Access is a device, a data plan, an interface that speaks your language, content that imagines your body, the skills to use it, and a household that lets you use it unwatched. This report follows sixteen women and queer activists with disabilities through all of it.

Content note

This report discusses sexuality, sexual expression, online harassment and sexual violence. Participants are quoted anonymously, by role. Accounts of harassment are kept behind a click.

Chapter I

A culture of silence

In a society where menstruation is hard to mention, the sexuality of a woman with a disability is not a difficult subject — it is an unthinkable one. Disabled bodies are read as asexual; the moment they express desire, they are read as hypersexual. There is no third option, so people choose the safer label.

Paternalistic notions of honour, respect and purity are attached to women across South Asia and used to police their sexual freedom and reproductive health. For women with disabilities a further layer of paternalism applies: they are regularly viewed as objects of pity, needing support, and incapable of agency. Globally that view has produced forced hysterectomies and forced abortions; the UN Special Rapporteur has warned that discriminatory laws and policies undermine the fundamental right of girls and young women with disabilities to exercise choice over their own bodies.6

Hypersexuality is a charge levied at any woman who admits to sexual desire, even inside marriage. For a woman of whom asexuality is the expected norm, the accusation sticks harder. Faced with that binary, participants chose the identity carrying less social risk — asexual — and avoided discussing themselves as sexual beings online at all. Several described it plainly as a survival strategy.

The policing does not only come from outside. Nepal’s disability rights movement was initiated and is still led mostly by Brahmin cisgender men; women’s issues within it are led mostly by Brahmin cisgender women. The movement’s agenda is built around what it counts as basic needs — education, employment, financial independence — with sex and sexuality kept secondary. Where sexual and reproductive health is raised, it is framed around heteronormative ideas of marriage and motherhood, so the fight to be seen as marriageable becomes the first step towards sexual rights, even though fixating on marriageability narrows the idea of autonomy.8

The law joins in5

Article 71.2 of the Civil Code 2017 allows a marriage to be voided where someone was ‘fraudulently’ married to a person who cannot speak or hear, is blind, or has a severe intellectual disability. A partner can later claim he did not know — which makes a consensual marriage prosecutable, against Article 23 of the UN CRPD.

Section 6 of the Privacy Act 2018 holds reproductive health information private, unless the person is of ‘unsound mind’ — in which case it may be given to family or a guardian. Privacy becomes conditional on a diagnosis, and the decision passes to whoever is deemed to hold guardianship.

The word itself is a barrier

The Nepali term for sexuality, younikta, is built on the root youn — sex — and carries an even narrower connotation than the English word. It is a recent import, absent from everyday speech, and already loaded with assumptions of decency and indecency. It also invites the accusation of imported activism.

There is not yet a Nepali word that holds beauty, appreciation, self-expression, self-exploration and reproductive health together. Participants were therefore answering a narrower question than the one being asked.

Why the reframing matters

If sexual expression is only ever understood as confessing bedroom details, then any discussion of sexuality feels far riskier than it is — and a great deal of expression that is already happening goes unrecognised, including by the people doing it.

Chapter III

The cost of being a role model

Activists are expected to be motivational. Sadness reads as negative, a photograph in a dress invites correction, and a kiss posted online has to be deleted — because the organisation is attached to everything you are. Self-censorship here is not caution. It is a job requirement.

Participants described a clear internal division between ‘good’ and ‘bad’ uses of the internet. Good meant sharing news from the disability rights community, their organisations’ events, their work. Bad meant personal information — especially anything sad or distressing — because leaders are supposed to inspire. One participant was told by the head of her organisation not to post pictures from a New Year’s club night, and then not to post personal pictures at all. She accepted the first instruction and refused the second.

The pressure also comes laterally, from colleagues in the movement, framed as being for the person’s own good. One participant recounted being told by fellow members that posting photographs with personal captions would be taken badly — and that she has been trying to post as little as possible since. Another explained why: followers would get the wrong message and be demotivated.

Where this lands is on sexual expression specifically. As one participant put it: being an activist makes you careful and conscious about what you post, so you do not post what you want to. She wanted to post a picture kissing her partner — “that is part of my sexuality” — and could not. Several described avoiding sharing content on sexual pleasure not out of disinterest but out of fear of losing credibility as activists.

Glossary

Terms as this report uses them.

Sexuality
The way people experience and express themselves sexually — biological, erotic, physical, emotional, social and spiritual feelings and behaviours.
Younikta (यौनिकता)
The Nepali term for sexuality, rooted in the word for sex, recently imported and commonly read as referring to the physical act alone.
Safe space
A place or forum where marginalised people can communicate their experiences without fear of being judged, trolled or attacked.
Microaggression
A small, often well-meant act carrying a demeaning assumption — here, most often the backhanded compliment.
Emotional labour
The unpaid work of managing other people’s feelings and curiosity about your body and your life.
Human rights model of disability
Recognises disability as part of human diversity, and locates exclusion in a disabling environment — replacing the charity model.
Charity model
Treats persons with disabilities as recipients of services designed for them and delivered to them, who should be grateful and content.
Intersectionality
The intersections in our identities, self-defined and imposed, and how they compound one another.
SRHR
Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights.
UN CRPD
The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006).

About

The study and the people behind it

Method

Sixteen in-depth interviews — fourteen women disability rights activists and two queer rights activists with disabilities — and two focus group discussions with 22 participants, all Kathmandu-based, all identifying as women. Guided by feminist ethical principles: consent beyond the form, discussants as experts in their own lives, and researchers reflecting on their own position.

Limits the authors name

Focus groups and in-person interviews are not anonymous and tend to produce more conservative answers on taboo subjects. Participants all own phones and live in the Valley, so this is a more formally educated, urban subset. Interviews were conducted by one researcher and written up by another from translated transcripts; some nuance will have been lost.
