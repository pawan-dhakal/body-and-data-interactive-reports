Mapping Laws

relevant to online violence in Nepal

Nepal has no single law for online gender-based violence. It has dozens of provisions that could be used — and most of them cut both ways. This report reads every one of them through three human rights lenses, and against fifteen real cases.12

Content note

This report discusses sexual and gender-based violence, including non-consensual sharing of intimate images, blackmail and threats. Case summaries are factual and use the report’s own pseudonyms; defendants are unnamed here. Case detail is kept behind a click throughout.

The one-sentence finding

Nepal does not need a new law for online violence so much as it needs the laws it already has to stop being usable against the people they were written to protect.

The framework

Three rights, read together

Every law in this report is examined through three human rights lenses. They are not separable: violating one damages the others.

Lens one

Freedom of expression

Guaranteed by the UDHR and the ICCPR, and by Articles 17(2)(a) and 19 of Nepal’s Constitution — then narrowed by restrictions written in the language of morality, decency and social harmony. Sexual expression by women and queer people draws the most scrutiny.8

104 press freedom violations recorded April 2018–2019; 106 cases logged in Kathmandu’s cyber crime unit for “improper social media posts” under the ETA.4

Lens two

Privacy

Article 12 of the UDHR, Article 17 of the ICCPR and Article 28 of Nepal’s Constitution — all with an “except as provided by law” attached. The public/private split the law relies on is itself gendered: what happens inside a home has long been treated as nobody’s business.9

The UN General Assembly recognised in 2016 that women are particularly affected by privacy violations in the digital age.3

Lens three

Freedom from violence

CEDAW defines violence against women as violence directed at a woman because she is a woman. Nepal’s Constitution criminalises physical, mental, sexual and psychological violence — but only the ETA names the online form of it, and only partially.

11,738 domestic violence cases recorded in FY 2076/77; in over 90% of registered online violence cases the victims are women aged 16–25.5

Case study · expression The first deportation over a social media post READ +

Robert Penner, a Canadian citizen working for a technology company in Nepal, wrote frequently on social media about political issues, including the rights of marginalised groups and the constitutional provisions restricting Nepali women’s right to confer citizenship. In 2016 he was held in custody for 26 hours after accusations of posting a provocative message aimed at spreading social discord, and was deported by the Department of Immigration. Authorities said he had been spreading unnecessary messages about Nepal, but did not say who had complained.7

It was the first case of a foreign national being removed from Nepal over social media posts. The positions Penner took were not new — they echoed the existing advocacy of many minority and women’s rights activists in Nepal. That is what made the removal effective: a chilling effect across the wider activist community, and encouragement for conservative politics.

Read together: perpetrators were mostly men known to the victim — current or former partners, friends, colleagues, acquaintances. Where convictions came, courts framed the harm as damage to the woman’s “honour”, “dignity” and “reputation” rather than as a violation of her privacy and bodily autonomy. Compensation was rare, and usually refused because no amount had been named in the papers. Punishments for online offences are, on paper, heavier than for their offline equivalents — up to five years and Rs 100,000, against one year and Rs 10,000 — with no stated reason for the asymmetry. Given the reach of online expression, one reading is that the stringency is aimed at speech rather than at violence.10

Chapter

Problems within the justice system

Before the law can fail a victim, the process usually does: mediation instead of prosecution, no private room to report in, evidence kept in an unlocked drawer, and ninety days to act.

Mediation between victim and perpetrator is treated as the first step towards “resolving” the problem rather than as a failure to address the victim’s needs. Police pressure people to negotiate and settle, and settlement generally means the victim is silenced or takes an unfair resolution.

Reporting itself exposes victims to further harm. There is no privacy at the point of filing — no separate room — so details must be given in front of other people. To register a cyber crime case the victim must hand over evidence, which may include leaked intimate images. During this research the Cyber Crime Unit was working out of a temporary tent shared with the Organised Crime Unit, and case folders containing that evidence were kept in a drawer with no lock.

The statute of limitations ranged from three months to a year depending on the offence — ninety days for domestic violence, workplace sexual harassment, breach of privacy, defamation and slander. Advocate Subin Mulmi noted that such limits come from evidence law, but that they can stop victims filing at all when psychological harm has left them unable to act in time. The Civil Code allows late filing only for natural calamity, accident or pregnancy; psychological conditions are not among them.

Technical incompetence compounded it. In several reviewed cases authorities confiscated computers when the harassment had been carried out from a mobile phone — a point the Appellate Court had to correct.

Glossary

Terms as this report uses them.

Online gender-based violence
Gender-based violence carried out or amplified through digital platforms — an extension of offline violence rather than a separate phenomenon.
Digital rights
Human rights as they are invoked in digitally networked spaces, including the agency a person exercises over their own expression and personal data.
Doxing
Publishing someone’s private identifying information without consent, usually to expose them to harassment.
Sextortion
Coercing sexual acts, images or money from a person by threatening to publish intimate material about them.
Non-consensual intimate images
Sexual or intimate images shared without the consent of the person in them, whether or not they consented to the image being made.
Chilling effect
When the mere possibility of legal action, rather than any prosecution, makes people stop speaking.
Statute of limitations
The window within which a complaint must be filed — ninety days for several of the offences relevant here.
CEDAW
The Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, to which Nepal is a signatory.
ETA
Electronic Transaction Act 2063 (2008) — the only Nepali act that explicitly criminalises online violence, and only partially.
Victim blaming
Shifting responsibility for violence onto the person harmed — described in this report as a recurring feature of hearings, not an exception.

Method

Desk review of six acts and bills through a gender lens; four key informant interviews with attorneys inside and outside government and the Cyber Crime Unit of Nepal Police (May–June 2018); and review of fifteen legal cases with case files, statements and hearings.
